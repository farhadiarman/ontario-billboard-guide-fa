What you own and what you rent

You lease the land from the landlord. You own the structure: the screen, the pole, the foundation, and the wiring.

RULE: Always write a permit-loss exit into the lease, a clause that lets you leave early if the town ever cancels your permit.

Three ways to earn

1. Own a board. Build your own face and sell the six slots yourself. This is the main plan in this guide. In a likely year, one board nets about $33,116 before loan payments (ESTIMATE). That is about $2,760 a month before the loan payment ($33,116 / 12). Part 2 has the full math, including a bad year and a good year.

2. Sell someone else's board. Become a rep: sell slots on a board someone else owns and keep a cut. Plan on 10% to 15% (ESTIMATE): no Ontario operator publishes a rep rate, but 80% of out-of-home companies pay reps 10% or less, and agency commissions run 15% to 17.5% (Billboard Insider, US trade sources; Part 13).

One board in Vaughan (BillboardHub listing #5107, 7800 Keele St) lists a slot at $1,750 for a 4-week period (28 days), the same price for 4, 12, 24 or 52 weeks (checked September 23, 2026). At 15%, that is $262.50 a period. $262.50 x 13 periods = $3,412.50 a year, for one slot sold all year. Three slots like that earn about $10,238 a year, with almost no cash of your own.

3. Do services for other boards. Work for a sign shop or LED installer. Trade experience from elsewhere (sign-making, electrical work, construction, or similar) transfers directly. Job Bank's Ontario wages for a Sign Installer (NOC 73200, 2023-2024 data) are $18, $25 and $40 an hour for low, median and high: $37,440, $52,000 and $83,200 a year at 2,080 hours. Sign makers (NOC 94219) are $17.60, $22 and $32.02 an hour. The Ontario government's Starter Company Plus grant pays up to $5,000 and needs a 25% matching contribution (Part 12), so this job likely pays more. You can save toward owning a board at the same time.

Tip: Many people start with way 2 or 3 to prove the business and build cash, then move to owning a board. Part 13 has a longer, ranked list of cheaper ways in.

Printed face versus digital face

Across Canada, printed boards are shrinking and digital boards are growing. ESTIMATE, based on the boards we researched.

Slot, loop, and the four-week period

A slot is one advertiser's time on the screen. This plan uses six slots per face. A loop is all six slots played in sequence, then repeating.

In Richmond Hill, each slot runs about 8 seconds: 6 x 8 = 48 seconds, so the loop repeats roughly every 48 seconds all day. Under East Gwillimbury's rules, the slot is 10 seconds: 6 x 10 = 60 seconds, so the loop repeats roughly every 60 seconds.

A 4-week period is the block advertisers buy, not a calendar month. 13 periods (52 weeks) cover one year.

Occupancy is the average share of slots sold across the year. At 60%, that is 3.6 of the 6 slots sold on a typical day.

Dwell time (the shortest time a slot must show) varies by town: Richmond Hill 3 seconds, Bradford 5 seconds, Newmarket 6 seconds, East Gwillimbury 10 seconds. VERIFIED against each town's own rule book. This plan uses 8- to 10-second slots, each at or above its town's minimum (8 seconds in Richmond Hill, 10 in East Gwillimbury).

Part 2. How the Money Works

In one sentence: In the Likely case one board leaves $33,116 a year before loans and tax and pays back a $175,000 build in about 5.3 years; in the Bad case it never truly pays back.

All dollars are Canadian. Checked as of September 21, 2026, from our own master money file. "Money left" is this guide's word for NOI: revenue minus land rent, sales cost and fixed running costs, before loan payments, tax and salary. Part 12 covers what happens to it after a loan.

Likely case, worked:

  • Full-year ceiling. 6 slots x $1,600 x 13 periods = $124,800, with every slot sold every period.
  • Real revenue. $124,800 x 60% full = $74,880.
  • Land rent: 20% of revenue or $10,000 a year, whichever is more. 20% of $74,880 is $14,976, above the $10,000 minimum, so rent is $14,976.
  • Sales cost: commonly 10% of revenue, paid to whoever sells the ads. 10% of $74,880 is $7,488.

  • Money left. $74,880 minus $14,976 minus $7,488 minus $19,300 = $33,116 a year.

The highway rule, and who actually gets highway signs

RULE: we will not plan a sign near a highway. It is not a real path for us.

RULE: across the whole province, MTO allows no billboard within 400 m of a 400-series highway, a fast highway with no stoplights: 400, 401, 404, 407 or 427. No city exception exists anywhere. A sign closer than 400 m needs a Minister's Order, signed personally by the minister in charge of highways: $770.00, valid 5 years, renewal required. The province can cancel it early and owes nothing back.

Every large freeway sign we could trace sits on government land, or land held by a government agency. Pattison runs signs like this at highway rest stops. Allvision has one near a GO train station (GO is the train system around Toronto); one example is not a pattern. Each operates under a Minister's Order, a path open to big companies that already know people in government, not to a new small business. One sign near Highway 400 and Steeles Avenue matched no public record, so exceptions cannot be ruled out; every sign we could check follows the rule.

Why Toronto is closed

Toronto bans a new billboard within 400 m of six highways: 400, 401, 404, 409, the Don Valley Parkway and the QEW (Queen Elizabeth Way). The Gardiner Expressway and Highway 427 are banned too, under their own separate rules. Another ban covers 450 m of much of Yonge Street. What is left is narrow strips of industrial and utility land, already crowded with existing signs.

A new sign there almost always needs a variance from Toronto's five-member Sign Variance Committee. Between 2024 and September 18 2026, the committee decided 28 requests for a new digital third-party sign and refused 18, about 2 out of every 3. The few approvals mostly went to companies with a lot of money already, whose land nearly met every rule on its own.

Toronto also charges an annual sign tax: $15,084.23 a year for a sign our size, about 20 cents of every dollar a board like ours earns. That is about two and a half months of sales, and it is owed before any ad is sold.

Elections. Ontario's municipal election is October 26 2026. New councils in most towns begin serving from mid-November 2026, right after you would start applying. A new council can ask staff to rewrite a sign by-law at any time.

Who you are dealing with, and how long they take

  • Family-owned lot. One or two decision makers. First reply in 1 to 3 weeks. Signed deal in 2 to 4 months.
  • Car dealership. The on-site landlord can say yes in 1 to 4 weeks, but the car brand's sign rules or the dealership's lender can still veto it. Total time is usually 2 to 5 months, or a fast no. A personal contact in that dealer network may open this door faster.

The size rule that comes before anything else

Being your own general contractor, safely

RULE: for a first board, hire ONE prime contractor. That is a turnkey supplier (one company that does most of the work) such as Media Resources, or one consultant named in writing to act as constructor, such as Rouge Hill Consulting.

Ontario safety law names one person in charge of site safety, the constructor, with real legal duties. Careful: if you hire more than one contractor, someone must sign as constructor. If nobody does, the law will likely name your company by default.

Becoming the constructor means three things:

  1. You must file a Notice of Project with the Ministry of Labour before work starts. This applies above $50,000 of labour and materials, which a board far exceeds.
  2. You must collect a valid WSIB clearance certificate from each contractor before work starts. Without one, you can be held responsible for a contractor's unpaid WSIB premiums.
  3. Neither owner should do the physical construction labour.

If no contractor will take the role, you end up as constructor. Expect paperwork and time, not extra money.

Pre-selling, before you build anything

RULE: never order the screen or the steel before two things happen: four of the six slots signed, and the town's permit in hand.

  1. Letter of intent (LOI): a one-page paper. The business says it would like a slot once the board is built, and it states a price. No money changes hands, and it does not bind the advertiser. It shows real interest, to you and to a lender.
  2. Signed order with a deposit: once the permit and the land lease are both signed, turn each LOI into a real order. The advertiser pays a deposit of 10% of the year's price. It is held in trust, in a separate account the company cannot spend, and refunded in full if the board never goes live. On a $1,400 x 13 year, that is $1,820 per slot.

Worked example: four signed slots at $1,400 each, for a full year, equals $72,800 of revenue ($1,400 × 13 periods × 4 slots). That is close to the plan's Likely case (the middle revenue estimate): 6 slots at $1,600, 60% full, $74,880 a year.

Give every advertiser real proof once the board is live:

Part 11. Setting Up the Business

Facts below are VERIFIED, checked September 21, 2026, unless marked otherwise. Nothing here is legal or tax advice.

The company

Incorporate under the Ontario Business Corporations Act (OBCA). Cost: $300, filed online, ready the same day.

RULE: incorporate in Ontario, not federally, and not as a partnership.

Why not the alternatives:

  • Federal corporation (CBCA): at least 25% of directors must be "resident Canadians." Ontario has no such rule.
  • Partnership: each partner can bind the other personally, and both carry unlimited personal liability.

Detail: CBCA section 105(3) sets the residency requirement. Section 2(1) defines "resident Canadian": a citizen who lives in Canada, or a permanent resident who lives here, but only up to one year past the date they became eligible for citizenship.

The bank account

Open a business account in the corporation's name right after incorporation. Bring a source of funds pack: a written explanation of where the starting money comes from, backed by documents.

If any startup money has passed through Iran, or sits in an account connected to Iran, plan for extra questions and time. Canadian banks must treat every transaction to or from Iran as high risk, at any dollar amount. They must ask where the money came from, verify identity, and report it.

DO: start with money already in a Canadian bank account with a clear history. Keep every paper: sale documents, old bank statements, salary records. Have them translated and certified if written in Farsi. Tell your bank in person, before a large amount arrives, not after.

DO NOT: split a deposit into smaller amounts to stay under the $10,000 reporting line. This is called structuring, and it is treated as suspicious on its own, whatever the real reason. Do not use an unregistered exchange house to move money, or an informal money network such as hawala. Running one without registering is a federal offence, and a customer can end up holding someone else's illegal money without knowing it.

HST

The employer consent note

You are a full-time employee elsewhere.

The shareholder agreement

The agreement should cover:

  1. Who does which job, and how many hours a week.
  2. How salary and dividends get decided.
  3. What happens if one person wants to leave, or cannot keep working.
  4. Who buys the other out, and at what price.
  5. How disagreements get settled, before they reach a courtroom.

If you are married, Ontario's Family Law Act treats company shares as property that can be divided in a separation. Otherwise a court decides later, so settle it in advance in a marriage contract or a clause in the shareholder agreement.

If you or your co-founder lends the company money, write it down clearly as either a loan or a gift. A documented loan is the cleanest way to get that money back out, and it avoids extra tax later. Charge at least CRA's prescribed rate: 3% for the last quarter of 2026. A gift is simpler but gone for good: it cannot later be taken back out of the company tax-free.

Detail: if you and your co-founder are related, whether by blood, marriage, or common law, TOSI (tax on split income) applies to both of you. TOSI can tax a dividend at the highest personal tax rate unless an exception applies. One exception: working 20 hours a week or more in the business, with timesheets to prove it. Whoever does not clear that hours test should take no dividends until the accountant confirms another exception, such as owning 10% or more of the shares. TOSI never touches salary, only dividends.

Source of funds for money from Iran

If any of your cash started in Iran, or passed through an Iranian bank account, treat it as a special case. This is not legal advice. See a Canadian sanctions and anti-money-laundering lawyer before any of that money moves, and a CPA.

What to do. Start with money already in a Canadian bank account with a clear history. It needs no special proof. Keep every paper record for money from elsewhere: sale agreement, property deed and bank statements, translated and certified. Tell your bank the story yourself, in person, with documents, before a large amount arrives. Use only money services businesses registered with FINTRAC, Canada's financial intelligence agency, and check its public registry yourself first. Report any cash of $10,000 or more crossing the border, using the CBSA (Canada Border Services Agency) declaration form.

What not to do. Never split a deposit into pieces under $10,000 to avoid a report. This is called structuring, and it is a warning sign on its own even when the money is clean. Never use an unregistered exchange house. Never use an informal hawala-style network either, even on a relative's word. It moves money person to person with no bank and no paper trail. Operating one without registration is a federal offence, and using one can leave you holding money that police later trace to someone else's crime.

Why banks ask so many questions. Banks must establish source of funds (how this money was earned or acquired: salary, gift, property sale) and source of wealth (how your whole net worth built up). Any transaction connected to Iran, of any size, is treated as automatically high risk. The bank checks identity, asks for source of funds, keeps a record and often reports the transaction.

PEP status can follow relatives too. A PEP (politically exposed person) is someone who held a senior government, military, judicial or state-company position. It covers close relatives, including a spouse, parent or in-law, and once it applies it never expires. If your co-founder, or anyone close to them, held such a position, tell your bank and lawyer up front.

Sanctions do not ban ordinary people or ordinary savings. Canada's Iran sanctions freeze dealings only with named individuals and organizations, including Iran's Revolutionary Guards. In practice, Canada's own travel advisory says money cannot be sent to Iran through normal banking today, so moving money out of Iran at all is a real practical problem.

T1135 is a tax form for money held outside Canada. It applies if you or your co-founder held more than $100,000 of foreign property at any point in a year, including a foreign bank balance. If so, it was due with that year's tax return. UNKNOWN: whether it was filed for past years. If it was missed, fix it before any large transfer, using the CRA's Voluntary Disclosures Program, which can reduce penalties for coming forward first.

A practical rule for advertisers, too. Check any currency exchange advertiser on FINTRAC's public registry every time before signing, because registrations get revoked. Accept payment only by cheque or bank transfer from the advertiser's own business account. Never accept cash. Add a clause to remove the ad if registration is lost.

Traps to watch for

  • The more than 50% rule blocks doubling up. Futurpreneur and Starter Company Plus each need their applicant to own more than half the company, so only one of you can use one of them.
  • You cannot use Starter Company Plus while working full-time at an outside job. It fits whichever of you does not.
  • The CSBFP's 85% guarantee protects the bank, not you. With a personal guarantee signed, you can still owe every dollar personally.
  • A big prepay discount is expensive money in disguise. 10% or 15% off for a year up front costs like paying 22% to 36% a year in interest. Offer a locked price instead.
  • A personal guarantee follows the person, not the corporation. The corporation shields you only from debts you did not personally guarantee. Never let your spouse or co-founder sign one without their own lawyer.
  • A dead board still costs real money. If the business fails, three costs remain: lease payments left, removal cost and any minimum rent owed. Together they can add up to $155,000 to $205,000, before tax relief. ESTIMATE: this number is likely too low for the bigger board this guide now plans. It was built on an older, smaller board and a smaller screen lease. Keep a bigger reserve if the lease and removal costs are not capped by contract.

The seven biggest risks, one at a time

1. Richmond Hill will not confirm the zoning

What it is. Richmond Hill's sign by-law allows a billboard on Commercial-zoned land, at least 100 metres from Residential land. Most Yonge Street lots sit in a mixed-use zone, and the by-law does not say whether that counts as Commercial or Residential. If the City reads it as Residential, zero of the 83 candidate lots on Yonge Street pass.

The fix. Before spending a dollar on design, send six written questions to the City's sign permit desk and get written answers. Email signpermits@richmondhill.ca, phone 905-771-8800. Also use the free zoning information line at zoninginfo@richmondhill.ca.

Stop rule. If the written answer says mixed-use counts as Residential, do not appeal or argue. Move to East Gwillimbury or Bradford. Part 4 explains why they are the back-up towns.

2. The landlord's own tenants, lender, or plans get in the way

What it is. Plaza tenants often have a right to their own sign panel on the same pole, and the landlord's bank may need to approve a new lease. Leases like this usually run 10 to 20 years. About 28% of the large Yonge Street lots already have a live City development application, so the site could be torn down for a taller building before your lease ends.

Worked example. A lease runs 15 years. The landlord sells the plaza for redevelopment in year six. Roughly $94,000 sunk into steel, power, and engineering could be lost, plus the roughly $20,000 to $42,000 still owing on the screen lease. This happens only if the lease has no protective clause.

The fix. Before signing anything, pay for a parcel-register search (a title search) on the chosen lot. It costs about $36.50 to $49 per lot. It shows the owner's name, any mortgage, any registered lease, and any site-plan agreement (the City's paper approving how the property was built). Write the lease with a right to relocate the sign if the property redevelops, and a payment if the sign cannot be moved.

Stop rule. Two red flags: the search shows a live tower application on the exact lot, or the landlord's lawyer will not agree to a relocation clause. Either one means walk away from that lot and pick another from the shortlist.

3. Power costs more and takes longer than planned

What it is. A 10 by 20 foot LED sign draws up to 15,500 watts at peak and averages about 4,000 watts through the day, roughly $6,100 a year of electricity. Alectra, the utility serving Richmond Hill, can ask for up to six months' notice, but only if new equipment must be built to reach the site.

The fix. File the power request as early as possible, no later than January 31 2027. Ask in writing whether the existing plaza pylon's circuit (the pole sign's existing wiring) can carry the extra load. That question alone can shrink the wait to one to three months.

Stop rule. Do not take the sign permit or order steel until Alectra's written connection offer is in hand, with its price and timeline confirmed.

4. Advertisers will not sign a year ahead of a board that does not exist

What it is. The plan's safety rule is to sell four of six slots before ordering steel. But small local businesses usually buy four to twelve weeks of advertising, rarely a full year, and rarely commit to a board that is not built yet. This is untested.

The fix. Use non-binding letters of intent for the pre-sale instead of year-long contracts. Offer a locked-in founding-advertiser price of $1,400 per four weeks to the first four advertisers who commit. Back it with proof from a real test ad, bought on someone else's board first, as Part 13 explains.

Stop rule. Count the letters of intent at Gate 1, January 31 2027 (Part 5). If fewer than four exist at $1,400 or more, do not move to Phase 1. Sell harder for another month, lower the price, or stay a reseller.

5. The build costs more than the ceiling

What it is. No verified written Canadian quote exists yet for a real 10 by 20 foot digital sign. Every number is an estimate, from $130,000 for a smaller, smart build up to $225,000 for the worst case. Add 8% for surprises ($18,000 on $225,000) and the real worst case to plan for is about $243,000. Steel and LED-screen prices are also exposed to the tariffs added through 2025 and 2026.

The fix. Get five written quotes in Canadian dollars before Gate 2. Ask every supplier to quote the same list of work, and to state how long the quote stays valid. Prices can expire in 30 to 60 days.

Stop rule. If every quote comes in above about $243,000 (the $225,000 stress case plus 8%), do not proceed with the full 10 by 20 design. Switch to the smaller 8 by 16 foot face. It needs about $44,000 less screen and half the road frontage.

6. Your co-founder's immigration status does not allow the planned work

What it is. Canadian immigration law can count unpaid, day-to-day work as work. Some statuses, such as a visitor's stay or a work permit tied to one named employer, do not allow someone to run or work in a Canadian company at all.

The fix. If your co-founder is not a Canadian citizen or permanent resident, get their status confirmed in writing by a licensed immigration consultant (RCIC, Regulated Canadian Immigration Consultant) or lawyer before they do any paid or unpaid work in the company.

Stop rule. If the written opinion says your co-founder cannot legally work in or run the company, restructure so you are the sole operator on paper. Pay your co-founder only once their status allows it, on legal advice.

7. A personal guarantee puts your home at risk

What it is. Banks, the government-backed small-business loan program, and equipment-lease companies often ask for a personal guarantee. The corporation does not protect anyone who signs one.

The fix. Only you, as an officer of the company, should ever sign a guarantee, with a top dollar limit written into it. Your spouse and your co-founder should never sign one casually. Never put a mortgage or lien on your home for the business. Keep the business bank account, insurance, and every signed document in the company's name only.

RULE: Your spouse and your co-founder should each get their own independent lawyer before signing anything a bank asks them to guarantee.

If it fails anyway, the recovery playbook

The worst realistic case, a total dead-board loss before any tax relief, is about $155,000 to $205,000. Your spending cap and cash reserve must cover this number, not only the amount sunk into steel (Part 2).

If a red signal fires, take these steps in order.

  1. Freeze spending. The same day, freeze all new spending. Write down every debt owed: the screen-lease balance, the loan, the rent left owing, and any prepaid advertisers. Cost is zero.
  2. Call the professionals. Within a week, book one hour each with the lawyer and the accountant. Do not move money to family, or sell equipment cheaply to friends, first. A court can undo a payment like that later as a preference (paying one creditor unfairly ahead of others) and can treat it as fraud. Cost is zero to $800.
  3. Talk to the lender. Call the screen-lease company and the bank before missing a payment. Ask for a payment holiday or an early buy-out number. This can save roughly $5,000 to $20,000 in what the lender would otherwise charge to collect.
  4. Tell advertisers. Tell them in writing and refund unused days. The most this ever costs is one prepaid period per advertiser, about $8,400 to $11,400 across all six slots.
  5. Try to sell it first. Sell the board as a going business before taking it apart. Potential buyers include Sanford Outdoor at 905-339-1339 and DigiPoint Media at 289-223-1001. Also try Eye Candy Ads at 647-896-4662 and Action Media at 705-687-2000. A working board can sell for about four to five times its yearly cash left. In a typical failure, that is roughly $78,000 to $97,000.
  6. Offer it to the landlord. Offer the landlord the structure in exchange for being released from removal costs. Removal otherwise runs $8,000 to $20,000.
  7. Dismantle if nobody buys it. Get two written quotes first. The likely cost is about $12,000.
  8. Close the company down. Settle small creditors, then file the final HST and corporate tax returns.
  9. Claim the tax relief owed. An ABIL (allowable business investment loss) lets you deduct part of the loss against your regular employment income. It is worth roughly 20 to 25 cents back per dollar lost, roughly $23,000 to $39,000 on a typical failed board.

Detail: Under the Canada Small Business Financing Program (CSBFP), the lender may take a personal guarantee up to the full original loan amount. This is set out in Justice Laws regulation SOR/99-141, section 19. VERIFIED. Under Ontario case law, a director has no automatic right to advice first. Get that advice anyway, on your own, before signing.

Dictionary

Ontario, Canada and billboard-industry terms used in this guide, A to Z. Dollar figures are Canadian dollars unless marked otherwise. Facts checked as of September 21, 2026.

A

  • AADT: Annual Average Daily Traffic, the vehicles past one point per day. York Region sells a count for $160 a road, and a 2016 to 2017 count on Yonge Street south of Elgin Mills found roughly 25,000 to 35,000 vehicles a day, both directions. A vendor's impression claim, such as 30,000 a day for 30,000 passing cars, is an estimate from counts like this, not proof.
  • AGCO: The Alcohol and Gaming Commission of Ontario. Its guideline says liquor ads should not sit within 200 metres of a school. This guide's advertiser rules follow it. No sign by-law writes it in.
  • Amendment: East Gwillimbury's spacing rule changed from 600 metres to 300 metres by amendment 2026-077, passed July 23, 2026.
  • Annual return: A yearly online filing to the Ontario Business Registry naming who runs the company and where its office is. It carries a small fee. Skipping it flags the company.
  • As-of-right: Allowed by the by-law as written, with only the ordinary permit. A sign that meets every distance and size rule can be as-of-right, and in East Gwillimbury staff can then approve it without a Council vote.
  • Attribution rules: Tax rules that shift tax back onto the person who gave or lent money when a family gift or loan moves income between people. A documented shareholder loan, backed by a promissory note, at CRA's 3% prescribed rate avoids them. An interest-free family loan could trigger them.

B

  • Bank of Canada policy rate: Also called the overnight rate. It is 2.25% now. Every bank loan in this guide is priced off it.
  • BDC: Business Development Bank of Canada, a federal lender that takes on more risk than a normal bank. Direct loans usually start only after 12 to 24 months of revenue, so it fits once the board is earning. It likes a credit score of 600 or higher, which a newcomer with no Canadian credit history may lack.
  • Break-even: At $1,600 a slot, about 26% full, or 1.6 of the board's 6 slots (Part 2).
  • Business Number: A 9 digit CRA number used for HST, payroll and corporate tax. You get one automatically when you incorporate in Ontario.
  • By-law: Richmond Hill's Sign By-law 25-26 made digital billboards legal there from April 8, 2026.

C

  • Caisson: The concrete-filled column that anchors a tall pole, dug below the frost line. A digital-sized foundation typically costs $5,000 to $20,000. A smaller, ordinary pylon's foundation costs about $3,000 to $8,000.
  • CASL: Canada's Anti-Spam Law, covering consent and unsubscribe rules for commercial email and text messages. An advertiser follow-up text must follow it. A first outreach text needs existing consent, or must clearly say who is messaging.
  • CBCA: The Canada Business Corporations Act, the federal incorporation law. Unlike Ontario's OBCA, it has historically needed some Canadian-resident directors.
  • CBO: See Chief Building Official.
  • CCA: Capital Cost Allowance, the tax write-off on equipment. A billboard's LED screen and steel structure fall under CCA Class 8, normally 20% of the balance left each year. Equipment bought from 2025 through 2029 gets a bigger 30% write-off in year one.
  • CCPC: Canadian Controlled Private Corporation, a private company mostly owned by Canadians. Only a CCPC gets Canada's low small business tax rate, on its first $500,000 of active business income. Advertiser rent counts as active business income, interest on idle cash does not.
  • CGL: Commercial General Liability insurance. Landlords and lenders typically ask for $2 million of coverage, with the landlord named as an additional insured before the lease is signed, costing roughly $800 to $2,500 a year. An umbrella policy up to $5 million might add around $1,000 a year.
  • Chargee: A lender with a registered claim, usually a mortgage, against land. Check the Parcel Register before any lease is signed. It shows the owner, any chargee (the bank that must sign off on a long lease) and any easement, such as a hydro easement across a plaza's back that can block a foundation spot. Ask that bank for a non-disturbance agreement, registered alongside the lease, so the lease holds even if the landlord defaults on their own mortgage.
  • Chief Building Official (CBO): The town's top building inspector. In Bradford West Gwillimbury, the CBO must give written approval first. Only then can a sign licence allow a digital screen.
  • CMS: Content management software for uploading ads and setting the schedule. Many screen makers, including Watchfire, bundle it free with the hardware.
  • Commercial zone: East Gwillimbury's by-law allows billboards on Commercial, Industrial, Employment, Rural or Aggregate Resource land. Residential land is not allowed.
  • Committee of the Whole: All of a town's councillors sitting together to discuss an item before the final Council vote. A sign by-law change is usually debated here first, then voted on at Council days or weeks later.
  • Competition Act: The federal law banning false or misleading advertising, with penalties that can run into large fines. Presenting an unverified traffic count as fact could breach it. That is why this guide labels traffic numbers as estimates.
  • Conservation authority: A regional agency whose permit is needed to build in a regulated area: land near rivers, valleys, wetlands and floodplains. In Richmond Hill, TRCA sign off must come before a building permit can be issued. See TRCA and LSRCA.
  • Constructor: Ontario workplace safety law names one party as constructor, legally in charge of safety on the site. Hiring several trades directly, instead of one prime contractor, will likely make your own company the constructor. One turnkey supplier, from design to final hookup, avoids that trap.
  • Controlled area: A strip of land near a provincial highway where the province, not the town, approves signs. A lot within Highway 404's controlled area needs an MTO permit before a billboard can go up. The strip includes a band around an interchange or intersection: 395 metres for a controlled-access highway and 180 metres for a King's Highway.
  • CPM: Cost per thousand impressions. Watchfire quotes $2 to $3 as the benchmark for a digital roadside board.
  • CRA: The Canada Revenue Agency, the federal tax office. It sets the prescribed rate for family loans and reviews large HST refunds before paying them out.
  • CSBFP: The Canada Small Business Financing Program, where the federal government backs 85% of a bank loan of up to $500,000 for equipment like a billboard's screen and structure, though you still owe it all. It charges a one time 2% registration fee and caps the interest rate at prime plus 3% (7.45% today), which already includes a 1.25% yearly administration fee. It usually needs a personal guarantee, capped at the loan amount.

D

  • Director: Ontario directors can carry personal responsibility for things like unpaid wages. You and your co-founder would both likely be named directors of the new corporation.
  • Double-faced sign: Allowed in Richmond Hill and East Gwillimbury. Its total sign area counts as twice the one-face limit, and the pole counts as one structure carrying two faces.
  • DSCR: Debt Service Coverage Ratio. Most lenders here want at least 1.25, meaning yearly money left is 1.25 times the loan payment.
  • Duty of loyalty: An employee's legal duty not to work against the employer's interests. Ontario's rules against non-compete agreements do not remove it. That is why the plan tells the employer about the side business in writing and gets the manager's written consent up front.
  • Dwell time: Richmond Hill requires at least 3 seconds. East Gwillimbury requires at least 10 seconds. Part 1 lists all four towns.

E

  • Election sign: A temporary sign supporting a candidate, under separate rules from billboard advertising. A candidate or registered third-party advertiser (a group running its own election ads) must give the billboard owner, called the "publisher" here, their information first. The publisher must keep the ad, its price and the sender's information for four years.
  • Employment zone: Vaughan only allows billboards inside its mapped Employment Areas: land set aside for factories, warehouses and offices, with no shops or homes.
  • Encroachment permit: Permission from York Region to place anything on or near its road land, even temporarily. An encroachment agreement costs about $2,300 and takes about 8 weeks. The plan avoids needing one wherever possible.
  • Equity injection: The cash a lender expects you to put in first. On a $175,000 build, roughly $60,000 to $80,000.
  • ESA: The Electrical Safety Authority, Ontario's electrical safety regulator. It charges a $143 fee to inspect a billboard's wiring, then issues the paper the utility needs before hookup. Only an ESA-licensed electrical contractor may legally do wiring for hire, and one must connect the billboard's power before ESA will approve the site.
  • ESTIMATE: One of this guide's three certainty tags. The number is this guide's own math from other verified numbers, arithmetic you can check yourself, not a quote or promise. The $175,000 build cost is ESTIMATE because no vendor has given a firm price yet.
  • Exclusivity: A lease clause promising the landlord will not let a competing sign company build nearby. Negotiate it to protect a Yonge Street pylon from a rival right next to it.

F

  • FFBA exemption: The Family, Friends and Business Associates exemption. An Ontario company can sell shares to close family and friends without registering with a securities regulator. A relative could put in about $43,300 for one third of a $130,000 board and receive about one third of its yearly money left.
  • FINTRAC: The federal agency that watches for money laundering. Money changing and money sending businesses must register with it. Rule: check every currency exchange advertiser against FINTRAC's public registry before signing them, since 18 Yonge Street registrations were revoked in 2026 alone.
  • Fixed running costs: $19,300 a year in this guide's model, whether the board is full or empty. They cover power (about 40,000 kWh, an average draw of roughly 4 kW), insurance, repairs, software, town fees, a property tax share and bookkeeping (Part 2).
  • Four-week period: Exactly 28 days, 13 a year. This guide's rate card and revenue model are both built on it (Part 1).
  • Frontage: Richmond Hill allows 0.30 square metres of sign area per metre of frontage. A full sized 19 square metre face then needs about 63 metres of frontage.
  • FSRA: The Financial Services Regulatory Authority of Ontario. It licenses mortgage brokerages and agents, including the independent mortgage brokers on the advertiser call list. The list favours them over bank mortgage specialists, whose employer usually controls their advertising budget.
  • Futurpreneur: A Canadian non-profit that lends money and provides mentors to business owners aged 18 to 39. Its Side Hustle loan can lend up to $25,000, and only to an owner who holds more than half the voting shares. It takes a 1% fee when the loan is paid out, typically allows interest-only payments for the first year, and pairs approved borrowers with a mentor for up to two years.

G

  • Gate: Gate 1 (January 31, 2027) and Gate 2 (about April 2027) are the go or stop checkpoints (Part 5). No steel or screen is ordered until 4 slots are signed at $1,400 or more per period, the permit is in hand and financing is approved.
  • Geotechnical report: A soil study, often a test hole, showing whether the ground can hold a foundation. It typically costs $3,500 to $8,000 and is needed before an engineer can design the caisson.
  • Grandfathered: Allowed to keep operating because the sign was legally there before the rule changed, also called legal non-conforming. Richmond Hill's grandfather clause only protects a sign already standing on November 9, 2009, so a board built new in 2027 has no automatic protection if the by-law changes again. A Newmarket sign put up before the current by-law may hold this status, but the Town would need to confirm it case by case.
  • Grant: No government grant found in this research pays for a billboard. Starter Company Plus can give your co-founder up to $5,000 toward startup costs, if they qualify. Its intake is currently closed, and the next one is expected in spring 2027, date unpublished.
  • GTA: The Greater Toronto Area, Toronto and the regions around it.

H

  • Heritage property: No digital screens are allowed on a property with Heritage protection, whatever the underlying zoning says.
  • HST: Harmonized Sales Tax, Ontario's 13% sales tax. The plan registers for HST on day one and chooses monthly filing, so refunds on business purchases come back roughly every month. Otherwise it can take up to a year.
  • Hydro: The everyday Ontario word for electricity. Connecting a new billboard means applying to the local distribution company, the utility that owns the poles and wires: Alectra in Richmond Hill and Vaughan, Newmarket-Tay Power in Newmarket.

I

  • Incorporation: Incorporating in Ontario costs $300, with Articles of Incorporation filed online. A national incorporation costs $200 plus a separate federal fee.
  • Independent legal advice: A bank may require a co-signing relative to meet a separate lawyer, who has no other role in the deal, before signing.
  • IRCC: Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada. An immigration lawyer or RCIC can get a written IRCC opinion on exactly what your co-founder's status allows them to do.
  • ITC: Input Tax Credit, the HST a business gets back on its own purchases. It needs HST registration first. The plan budgets an HST float of about $9,750 on the build's non-screen spending, refundable in roughly 2 to 3 months, though CRA can take longer on a large first refund.

L

  • Land Registry: OnLand, Ontario's Land Registry website, is a free way to find a parcel's owner by address. Start there with any shortlisted Yonge Street parcel.
  • Land rent: The greater of 20% of gross revenue or $10,000 a year. At $74,880 of revenue, 20% is $14,976, so that is the rent paid.
  • Land transfer tax: A one time Ontario tax on a land sale or a very long lease. A lease of 50 years or less, counting renewals, is exempt. This guide's leases run much shorter anyway, to satisfy a tighter Planning Act rule (see Notice of lease).
  • Late fee: A late fee clause is only enforceable if the contract states the yearly rate, for example 24% a year. Without a stated rate, the law caps it at 5% a year.
  • Letter of intent (LOI): A short, unsigned, non-binding statement that an advertiser wants a slot once built. It costs the advertiser nothing, still proves real interest to a lender, and helps clear Gate 1. Once the permit and lease are both in hand, it becomes a signed insertion order (a signed purchase of ad space, with time and price), backed by a deposit of 10% of the year's price, held in trust and refundable until the board goes live.
  • Licence: Bradford West Gwillimbury issues a 10 year sign licence. Other towns use shorter permits, and East Gwillimbury's renewal is entirely at the Town's discretion, not an automatic right.
  • Likely case: The guide uses three cases: Bad, Likely and Good. Likely assumes 60% of slots are sold on average across the year, 3.6 of 6. On the $175,000 build, it pays back in about 5.3 years.
  • Listed person: A person or company named on Canada's sanctions list. No one may legally deal with them, so no advertiser on the sanctions Schedule 1 list may be sold a slot. This matters given Canada's sanctions on Iran.
  • Loop: With 6 slots of about 8 seconds each, one loop takes roughly 48 seconds (Part 1).
  • LSRCA: The Lake Simcoe Region Conservation Authority. It covers the part of East Gwillimbury outside TRCA's area, so a build there needs LSRCA sign off, not TRCA's.

M

  • Metrolinx: The provincial agency that runs GO train and bus service and owns the land around its stations. Some freeway-facing billboards already stand on Metrolinx land under a Minister's Order.
  • Minister's Order: A written decision by a provincial minister creating a special exception to the normal rules. Freeway billboards on land like Metrolinx GO station property usually exist under one, in place of the normal municipal permit process.
  • Ministerial Directive on Iran: A federal order making Canadian banks treat any Iran money transfer as high risk and report it, regardless of amount. Even a fully legal transfer can trigger bank questions.
  • Minor variance: A small exception to a by-law that does not weaken its overall purpose, sometimes approvable by staff instead of a full committee. Barrie lets its town Clerk approve a minor variance without a full public meeting.
  • Mixed use zone: Much of Richmond Hill's Yonge Street is zoned mixed use. The town has never confirmed in writing whether that counts as Commercial land. This is the single biggest open question in this plan.
  • Money left: This guide's plain word for what remains after land rent, sales cost and fixed running costs, before any loan payment, tax or salary (industry term: NOI). In the Likely case, $74,880 of revenue leaves $33,116 a year (arithmetic in Part 2). A single owned board sells for 4 to 7 times that, roughly $132,000 to $232,000.
  • MPAC: The Municipal Property Assessment Corporation, which sets each property's value for property tax. MPAC adds the billboard to the landlord's assessment at replacement cost less 50%, so this guide's lease reimburses the landlord for that extra tax.
  • MSB: Money Services Business, a company that exchanges or sends money for a fee, such as a currency exchange (a sarafi in Farsi; Taheri Exchange at 5775 Yonge Street is one). Any MSB must be registered with FINTRAC. This guide's rule caps currency exchange advertisers at 2 of 6 slots.
  • MTO: The Ministry of Transportation of Ontario, in charge of provincial highways like the 400 series. It must approve any sign near one. MTO's rule bans billboards within 400 metres of a 400 series highway, with no exception for urban areas.

N

  • Nits: East Gwillimbury's by-law caps a digital sign at 5,000 nits in daytime. At night the cap is only 300 nits.
  • Notice of lease: A short public record at the Land Registry showing that a lease exists, which a new buyer or lender must then respect. This guide writes site leases as 21 years less a day, which satisfies a Planning Act rule needing town consent for any longer term.
  • Notice of Project: A form filed with Ontario's Ministry of Labour before construction starts, required when labour and materials cost more than $50,000. A 10 by 20 foot billboard costs far more than that, so the constructor must file this notice before work begins.
  • NUANS report: A search confirming no other Canadian company uses the exact name you want. A numbered company, such as 1234567 Ontario Inc., skips it entirely.

O

  • OBCA: The Ontario Business Corporations Act, the provincial incorporation law. Unlike the federal CBCA, it needs no Canadian-resident directors. That is one reason this guide recommends Ontario incorporation.
  • Off-premises sign / third-party sign: Ontario by-laws use both terms for a sign advertising a business not located on the land where it stands. This is the legal category a billboard falls under, so search by-laws for either term.
  • Offer to Connect: A power company's written price and plan for hooking up a new electrical service. Alectra's typically arrives within 60 days of a formal request. The plan calls for asking for a free pre-check even earlier.
  • Official Plan: Newmarket is currently rewriting its Official Plan, the town's long-term land-use plan, which could eventually affect which areas allow billboards.
  • OLS: An Ontario Land Surveyor. An OLS survey, a signed drawing of property lines and proposed sign placement, is needed before staff review a sign permit application. It costs roughly $2,000 to $4,500 and proves a billboard's exact distance from a lot line or another sign.
  • Ontario Business Registry: The government website where Ontario businesses register and file yearly reports. An $8 Corporation Profile Report shows a company's directors. It can show who owns a shortlisted parcel.
  • Ontario Land Tribunal: The provincial body that hears disputes over municipal planning decisions. Some large Yonge Street lots have active development applications that could end up before it. That is one reason this guide treats a long lease there as risky.
  • Ontario One Call: A free service that marks buried pipes and wires before anyone digs. The call takes 5 business days to process. The result then stays valid for 60 days.
  • Open work permit: A work permit letting its holder work for almost any employer in Canada. Public guidance does not clearly say whether it also covers running one's own business. Get a written RCIC opinion first, and do not assume it covers managing a new company.
  • Option to lease: Signed before the permit, so the option holds the land while the town decides. No rent is paid until the City confirms the sign is allowed, and the full lease follows only after the permit. Include a permit-loss exit, so walk-away cost is nil if the permit is refused.
  • Out-of-home (OOH): Total Canadian out of home spending was about $842 million in 2025, and 58% of it was digital.

P

  • P.Eng: Professional Engineer, licensed in Ontario to sign and stamp structural drawings. A P.Eng must stamp the drawings for a billboard's monopole and caisson.
  • PEP: Politically Exposed Person, someone who holds or once held a senior government or military role. Close family counts too, including parents-in-law, and the status never expires. Banks give them extra scrutiny, which matters for any banking involving your co-founder's family.
  • Permanent resident: Someone allowed to live in Canada permanently who is not yet a citizen, often shortened to PR. Most of the loan and grant programs here require citizenship or PR status.
  • PIN: Property Identification Number, the 9 digit number Ontario's land registry uses to identify one parcel. Searching an address on OnLand returns the PIN, the first step before buying a Parcel Register.
  • Pixel pitch: A 10 millimetre pitch is common and sharp for a roadside board. A 13 millimetre pitch is a little less crisp, but can save $20,000 to $40,000. At the chosen pitch this guide's screen is about 300 by 600 pixels, a number to confirm with your own supplier first.
  • Prescribed rate: The minimum interest rate CRA expects on a loan between family members, reset every three months. It is 3% for October to December 2026. A family loan charging less risks being treated as a taxable benefit.
  • Prime rate: Prime is 4.45% today. A CSBFP loan is capped at prime plus 3%, which works out to 7.45%. Reference case: a $150,000 bank loan over 10 years at 7.45% costs about $21,319 a year.
  • Programmatic: Platforms such as Broadsign Reach and Vistar sell ad space automatically. They only make sense with several boards, so this plan skips them for the first board. Once several boards are owned, unsold space (remnant) can be listed on Broadsign Reach for small extra revenue.
  • Proof of play: Watchfire's Ignite software prints a time stamped report for every play, showing an advertiser exactly when and how many times their ad played.
  • Protected person: Someone Canada has formally decided cannot safely return home and is allowed to stay. This is similar to a refugee, who can usually get an open work permit. An RCIC should confirm a protected person's right to run a business.
  • Pylon: A tall pole mounted sign at the edge of a parking lot, also called a pole sign. A rebuilt plaza pylon counts as a ground sign, meaning a sign on its own posts, not attached to a building. The plan's cheapest path is rebuilding an existing lit plaza pylon into a monopole (a single steel pole, no cross bracing) carrying a digital LED screen.

R

  • Rate card: This guide's rate card runs from $1,900 for a 4 week slot down to $1,500 for a 52 week slot. Founding advertisers are locked at $1,400 for their first year. Agencies get 15% off, so a $1,600 slot costs an agency $1,360.
  • RCIC: A Regulated Canadian Immigration Consultant, licensed to give immigration advice. An RCIC can give you a written opinion stating exactly what your co-founder's status allows them to do.
  • Regional road: A main road owned and controlled by York Region rather than the local town. Yonge Street is a Regional road, so York Region, not only Richmond Hill, has a say over signs and permits near it.
  • Rep agreement: A fair one asks for around 15% of what is collected, paid within 30 days, with no long non-compete clause and a right to leave with 30 days' notice. Working as a rep before owning a board costs almost nothing and is a fast way to learn real prices.
  • Request for quotation (RFQ): This guide's plan sends the same one page request to at least five builders, then chooses one. It asks each supplier to show freight, customs brokerage and duty separately from the screen's own price.
  • Reseller: Sanford Outdoor and DigiPoint Media both resell space owned by other companies. Both operate in this guide's research area.
  • Residency obligation: A permanent resident must spend 730 days in Canada every five years. Missing that risks losing PR status. It is one more reason to confirm your co-founder's immigration history in writing.

S

  • Sales cost: 10% of what advertisers bring in, paid to whoever sold the ads. On $74,880 of revenue, that is $7,488.
  • Sanctions: Canada's Iran sanctions freeze the property of listed people and companies. They do not ban ordinary Iranian citizens from moving their own money. Banks still treat any Iran linked transfer as high risk.
  • Screen lease: Lease-to-own means monthly payments for a set number of years, then you own the screen for $1. Several screen makers, including Daktronics and some in Canada, offer it directly (a vendor lease). Reference case: leasing $100,000 over 84 months at 10% costs about $19,921 a year.
  • Setback: Richmond Hill keeps a new billboard at least 100 metres from land zoned Residential and at least 400 metres from a highway.
  • Shareholder agreement: Often called a unanimous shareholder agreement. You and your co-founder should include a buy-sell clause covering what happens if one of you wants out.
  • Sign permit: Richmond Hill's costs $279 and includes the Building Code review needed for a structure this size, so there is no separate building permit fee. It lapses 6 months after issue if nothing has been built yet.
  • Sign Variance Committee: Toronto's five-member body that decides sign variances. Between 2024 and September 2026, it refused 18 of 28 digital sign requests, about 2 of every 3.
  • Site plan: Under site plan control, the town reviews a drawing of a property's buildings, driveways, parking and signs before something can be built or changed. Adding a sign to a plaza under site plan control may need a minor amendment, roughly $1,088 to $1,256 in Richmond Hill.
  • Small supplier: A business earning $30,000 or less in sales over the last four calendar quarters, not legally required to register for HST. This guide's plan registers for HST voluntarily from day one, to recover input tax credits.
  • Smart build: The cheapest of the three build-cost cases, $130,000. It uses a smaller screen and reuses the landlord's existing pole.
  • Soft costs: Survey, engineering, legal and government fees added together, separate from the steel and screen. On a clean Richmond Hill site, they run about $9,400. On a harder site, they can reach about $27,600.
  • Source of funds: Proof of exactly how the money in one specific deposit was earned or obtained, such as a pay stub, a property sale or a signed gift letter. A bank asking about a large deposit wants this, and a general description is not enough. A gift letter should state it is not a loan and show where the money came from.
  • Source of wealth: An explanation of how a person built up their money over their whole life, such as decades running a business or years of salary and savings. Enhanced due diligence on Iran money asks about both sources.
  • Special Signage Area: A named area, like a historic downtown, with tougher sign rules to protect its look. Digital screens are usually banned there. Richmond Hill's Downtown and Uptown areas are Special Signage Areas this plan avoids.
  • Sponsorship: Tirgan, a Toronto Persian arts festival, sells sponsor packages. So do Eventorian's Mehregan, Yalda and Nowruz events. Persian promoters run Mehregan parties across the GTA each fall, and Persian shops, grocers and restaurants advertise heavily around Nowruz and Yalda, a good time to renew or upsell advertisers.
  • Start-up reserve: At least $30,000 kept untouched as working capital. It covers the board's first uncertain months.
  • Static: Some builders put up the pole, foundation and wiring first as a printed static sign, protecting their spot until the digital screen is added.
  • Stress build: The worst-case build-cost case, $225,000, if steel and screen prices run high. It still pays back in the Likely case, in about 6.8 years, but never pays back in the Bad case.
  • Structuring: Deliberately breaking a large cash deposit into smaller pieces to stay under $10,000. Banks treat structuring as suspicious on its own, no matter the reason, and will report it.
  • Study permit: A government paper letting a foreign student study in Canada. It may allow limited work, often 24 hours a week off campus. A study permit alone would not be enough to actively run this business, so your co-founder's actual status needs confirming.

T

  • T1135: A tax form listing foreign property, such as bank accounts or land outside Canada. It is required if foreign property topped $100,000 Canadian in the year. Confirm this for your co-founder.
  • T2: The corporate income tax return every Canadian corporation files each year. A company's first T2 is usually prepared by its accountant and covers its first fiscal year.
  • TOSI: Tax on Split Income, a federal rule that can tax family members at the top rate on company income if they are not genuinely active in the business. It applies to you and your co-founder here. A family member drawing dividends without real hours worked risks the top rate.
  • Transition time: How long one digital ad takes to change to the next. Several Ontario by-laws expect about 1 second.
  • TRCA: The Toronto and Region Conservation Authority, covering Richmond Hill. TRCA, not LSRCA, is who to call about a regulated area in Richmond Hill.
  • Trim: The metal edging around the screen, also called the bezel. Richmond Hill's 19 m2 cap counts the trim, so a true 10x20 face needs a trim no wider than about 23 mm to fit under it.

U

  • Unmetered service: An electrical connection for something drawing a steady, predictable amount of power, with no meter and an estimated bill. Some smaller signs qualify, which can simplify the utility hookup.
  • UNKNOWN: One of this guide's three certainty tags: nobody has confirmed the fact yet, and the guide says how to find out. Whether your co-founder's work permit covers running their own business is marked UNKNOWN.
  • Uptime: An uptime of 95 to 98 percent is a normal industry benchmark, so up to about 14 to 36 hours dark a month is still normal.

V

  • Variance: A Richmond Hill sign variance costs $2,312 plus a $415 first notice fee. If it is refused, there is no built-in appeal, only a lawsuit called judicial review.
  • VERIFIED: One of this guide's three certainty tags. Someone on this project read the fact themselves, in an official document or a signed by-law. Richmond Hill's $279 sign permit fee is marked VERIFIED.
  • Visitor: Someone in Canada for a short stay who is not permitted to work. A visitor cannot legally help run this business, even unpaid, if a Canadian could otherwise be hired for the work.

W

  • Ward councillor: The elected politician for the neighbourhood where a sign is proposed. A ward councillor can ask for a granted sign variance to be reconsidered, within a set number of days after it is approved.
  • Work permit: An employer specific work permit only allows work for one named employer. It likely would not cover someone helping run their own new company.
  • WSIB: Ontario's Workplace Safety and Insurance Board. Subcontracting the install work can keep the company at the lower Class L rate, $0.18 per $100 of payroll, instead of Class G5's $2.15. Ask every subcontractor for a clearance certificate, proof they paid their premiums, so their unpaid premiums cannot become your bill.

Y

  • York Region: The upper tier government above local towns like Richmond Hill and Newmarket. It runs major roads such as Yonge Street and Davis Drive. The Region must be asked about signs and permits there too, not only the town.

Z

  • Zoning by-law: A sign by-law usually allows a billboard only on Commercial or Industrial zoned land. If it does not clearly allow one, a billboard is not allowed.
  • Zoning confirmation letter: Richmond Hill offers a free zoning information letter, taking up to 5 business days. Keep it as written proof. It is the first, cheapest step toward answering the Yonge Street zoning question.

2. The landlord offer letter

When to use it: once you have a shortlisted parcel with a pylon or room for one, before you spend on drawings. It is not a lease.

Subject: A proposal for the sign pole at [ADDRESS]

Dear [Landlord name],

My name is [YOUR NAME]. I am building a small, local digital sign company based right here in Richmond Hill. I am writing about the [pole / plaza sign] at [ADDRESS].

The idea. We would rebuild your sign pole into a modern digital sign, at our cost. We would keep every one of your tenants' panels exactly as they are today.

What you would receive. Rent equals 20% of gross advertising revenue, or $10,000 a year, whichever is more. Gross advertising revenue means all the money the sign takes in, before our costs. You get this rent no matter how full the sign is in a slow month. You would pay nothing toward building it. You would pay nothing toward its power, insurance, permits or upkeep either. Suppose the City raises your property's assessed value because of the sign. Assessed value is the dollar figure the City uses to set your property tax. We reimburse that extra tax too. About $1,000 a year in our plan, an ESTIMATE.

What stays protected. Your tenants' panels, sightlines and existing lease rights are unaffected. Brightness and content follow the City's rules and our own written policy. No flashing, no scrolling, static messages only.

Your exits. The lease term, including every renewal, would be written as under 21 years. This follows the Ontario Planning Act. Suppose you ever sell or redevelop the property. You can end the lease on notice, with fair payment for the structure's unamortized cost. This is the part of the build cost we have not yet earned back. Or we relocate the sign elsewhere on your property. If the sign business ever stops, we remove the structure at our cost. We post security up front to guarantee this.

Your protection. We would carry commercial general liability insurance, called CGL, of at least $2,000,000. It would name you as an additional insured. We would register the lease against the property. A future buyer would be bound by it. We would pay your own lawyer's and lender's fees to review and consent to it.

Next step. I would like 30 minutes to walk the site with you. I can show you photos of similar signs nearby, and answer any questions. Nothing needs to be decided at that meeting. Could you also tell me your lawyer's name? And whether your lender would need to know?

Thank you for considering this,

[YOUR NAME] [phone] [email]

Careful: never promise the town will approve the permit. Never state a guaranteed revenue number in this letter or out loud. Say "we expect" or "our plan assumes," never "you will earn."

If the landlord raises a concern:

What they worry about What you say
"This will raise my property taxes." Only the sign itself is assessed, at its replacement cost less 50%, by MPAC, the Municipal Property Assessment Corporation, Ontario's assessment body. Your land and building keep their own value. We reimburse the sign's share, about $1,000 a year in our plan.
"My tenants will lose their panels." Every panel stays. We get written tenant consent where a lease requires it.
"What if I sell or redevelop?" You can end the lease with 12 months' notice, plus payment for the unamortized cost. Or we relocate on your property. The lease is registered, so a buyer is bound by the same terms.
"What if your company disappears?" We post a removal deposit or bond. We carry $2 million liability insurance naming you. The lease can be assigned to a bigger, established operator if we ever need to step back.
"My lender will object." We prepare the consent package and pay your lender's and lawyer's fee. We ask only for a non-disturbance clause, a promise that your lender will not evict us if you default on your mortgage.
"Why should I believe a new company?" We are local, based on Yonge Street. We can show you signed letters of intent from advertisers and our insurance certificate before you sign anything.

3.1 Cold-call or walk-in script, 60 to 90 seconds

When to use it: your first approach to a local business owner, by phone or walking into their shop.

Open: "Hello, is this [Name]? I am [YOUR NAME]. I am not selling anything today. Do you have 60 seconds? If it is a bad time, tell me and I will call back." Why I am calling: "I am planning one digital billboard on Yonge Street, Richmond Hill, near [cross street]. It is not built. The town has not approved it yet. Before I spend money, I am asking ten local owners one thing. Would this be useful to you, at what price?" Why you: "I picked [Business] because [you are close by / customers pass here / we share a language]." Offer: "There are six slots. Each advertiser gets one slot of 8 seconds, all day. The founding price is $1,400 for 4 weeks, on a one-year order. That price is fixed for the year. You pay nothing until the board is live. A refundable deposit comes only after the town approves it." Ask: "Could I show you a picture of what your ad would look like on it? Ten minutes, [Tuesday or Thursday]. Morning or afternoon?" If yes: confirm day, time, place. Ask if they prefer WhatsApp or email for the picture. Write down the answer and the date. That is their permission to message them. If not now: "Understood. May I send a short message with the location and price?" Note the date. If no: "Thank you for your time. One question: what would make a sign like this worth it?" Then stop there. Voicemail: "Hello [Name], this is [YOUR NAME]. I am planning a digital billboard on Yonge Street in Richmond Hill. I would value your advice as a local owner. I will try again on [day]. You can reach me at [phone]. Thank you."

RULE: never say "guaranteed," "sold out soon" or "everyone will see it." Never claim false approval. A false claim breaks Canada's false-advertising law.

3.4 Follow-up sequence

When to use it: after any first contact that did not end in a signed LOI. Stop after day 45, unless they ask you to keep going.

  • Day 0: call or visit. Log who decides, best day to reach them, and what they said.
  • Day 2, message: "Thank you for your time, [Name]. Here is the picture we talked about: [image]. If anything is unclear I am happy to explain."
  • Day 5, email: a one-page summary. Location, size, slots, price, and the traffic count. Say the deposit is refunded in full if the permit fails.
  • Day 10, call with one new fact: "The Region's count is in. About [number] vehicles a day. Would ten minutes this week help?"
  • Day 21, message: "The founding price stays open until [date], or until advertiser four signs. Would you like a slot held for you?" Say this only if it is true.
  • Day 45, note: "I understand the timing may not be right. May I check in again in [month]? If plans change, my number is [phone]." Then contact every 3 months after that.

When to use it: keep this open during any sales call.

I will give you the count report to check it."
  2. "Is it worth it?" "I cannot promise sales, and I will not. I can set it up so we can see. Use a phone number or promo word only for the board. Ask your staff how each new customer heard about you. If one customer is worth $[profit] to you, divide $1,400 by that number. That tells you how many new customers you need in 4 weeks to break even. Example: a customer worth $200 to you needs 7 new customers in 4 weeks. That is $1,400 divided by $200. Does that sound possible?"
  3. "Too expensive." "Tell me what would feel comfortable. [Listen.] Options: 12 weeks at $1,750, a year at $1,500, $1,400 founding. That is about $50 a day, day and night. What am I not seeing that makes it feel high?"
  4. "I will think about it." "Of course. What would you like to think about: price, location, or whether it suits your business? If there was anything in this proposal you would change, what would it be? May I call you on [day] to hear your decision, whatever it is?"
  5. "I already advertise in [paper, radio, Instagram]." "Good, keep it. This is different, because people see it every day on the road, without choosing to. Many owners use it alongside what they already do. What do you spend now, and which month brings the most customers?"
  6. "It is not built. Why trust you?" "You should be careful. That is why nothing is due until it is live. The deposit is refundable and held in trust. If it is not live by [outside date], the order ends. Your deposit comes back. I will show you the permit status every month."
  7. "People drive too fast to read it." "True, for long messages. So we keep it to about seven words: your name, and one thing. It works best for being remembered, on a road people use every day."
  8. "I must ask my partner or head office." "Of course. Shall I prepare a one-page summary for them? Could we all meet on [day]?"
  9. "Can I try one month?" "Yes, once it is live. One period is $1,900, and the founding year is $1,400. Many owners find one month too short to judge fairly. Would 12 weeks at $1,750 be a fair middle?"
  10. "Give me a better price." "$1,900 minus $1,400 is $500. $500 is 26% of $1,900. I can talk about paying a year in advance." Only if you allow prepay discounts.
  11. "What if the town says no?" "Then the order ends. You get your deposit back in full, within 10 business days. It is written in the order."
  12. "The big companies are cheaper." "For a big reach, they may be. This board is one road in your neighbourhood. Ask what they charge for the road your customers use. Compare the cost per thousand views."
  13. "Do you have other advertisers?" "Not yet. You would be one of the first four. That is why the price is lower and fixed."
  14. "Have you done this before?" Say only what is true. Example: "I sell for a living. I have not run a billboard before. Nothing is due until it is live."

7. Co-founder agreement checklist

When to use it: before you incorporate or spend real money. Go through it with your co-founder and any family member with a financial stake.

People and status

  • [ ] Immigration status: if your co-founder is not a Canadian citizen or permanent resident, get their status confirmed in writing, by a paid RCIC (a Regulated Immigration Consultant) or an immigration lawyer, not IRCC's phone line, before they do any paid or unpaid work in the company. Status decides whether they can be paid, work unpaid, or only lend money and hold shares.
  • [ ] Personal details: your co-founder's age, when they landed in Canada, their town, and their English level.
  • [ ] PEP check: if your co-founder or a family member ever held, or is related to someone who held, a top government or military post abroad, that makes them a foreign PEP. That status is permanent once it applies, and it must be disclosed to any bank early.
  • [ ] Sanctions check: whether any bank you or your co-founder used in another country is on Canada's sanctions list. Check with a lawyer before any transfer.
  • [ ] T1135 check: whether your co-founder, or any family member with foreign assets, has ever needed to file a T1135 form with the CRA, and whether they actually have.

Money and risk

  • [ ] Total cash: is the cash you and your co-founder could put in already in a Canadian bank account with a paper trail?
  • [ ] Maximum loss: how much you could afford to lose completely if the business fails. A dead board costs an estimated $155,000 to $205,000 before any tax relief.
  • [ ] Money from a high-scrutiny country: whether money from a country with sanctions exposure or heavy bank scrutiny will be part of this. If so, it must move in one documented step, after a lawyer's advice.
  • [ ] Cash reserve: the minimum cash reserve you agree to keep untouched. At least $30,000. Or $45,000 to $60,000, if ending the screen lease early costs a lot.

Roles and hours

  • [ ] Who leads: who leads day-to-day decisions, and who has the final say if the two of you disagree.
  • [ ] Your co-founder's hours: their real weekly hours available. At steady state, with slots sold, expect 2 to 3 hours a week.   • [ ] Your hours: your real weekly hours alongside your regular job. At steady state, expect 3 to 5 paperwork hours, plus 2 to 6 selling hours. It is 12 to 18 hours combined, while the loop is under 60% full.
  • [ ] Spouse or family member's role: whether your spouse, or another family member with a financial stake, will hold shares or do regular work in the company. If they do, it changes your TOSI answer too. Ask the accountant.

Company structure

  • [ ] Share split: agree on the share split between you and your co-founder. Do this before incorporating, in writing, with a lawyer.
  • [ ] One main applicant: only ONE of you can be main applicant, Futurpreneur or Starter Plus. Both need over 50% ownership.
  • [ ] Who signs: agree in advance who signs contracts with the town, suppliers and advertisers. The research plan assumes this is you, since the paperwork is in English.

Decisions to make before you spend money

  • [ ] Ten open questions: answer the ten open questions in Part 15, Questions We Need Answered, and write the answers down where you both can see them.
  • [ ] Stop rule: agree now what you do, if under 45% full at 12 months live. Cut the price, sell the business, or wind it down.
  • [ ] RULE: order no steel or screen until four of six slots sell, permit in hand.

8. Bank meeting checklist

When to use it: before any meeting with Futurpreneur, Alterna, BDC, or a CSBFP-loan bank. CSBFP is the federal Canada Small Business Financing Program; the government guarantees most of the loan. Lenders want slightly different documents, so bring everything and let them say what they do not need.

Bring to every lender meeting

  • [ ] Business plan: include the numbers from Part 2, How the Money Works.
  • [ ] Cash flow forecast: month by month. Futurpreneur wants 24 months. A CSBFP bank, following CIBC's checklist, wants at least 1 year, month by month. It also wants a pro forma balance sheet.
  • [ ] Tax returns: T1 returns, and CRA Notice of Assessment, for the last two years. For both of you, and your spouse too, if the lender wants them as guarantor.
  • [ ] Net worth statement: a personal net worth statement for each guarantor. Bring investment account statements (RRSP retirement, TFSA tax-free and RESP education accounts) and a mortgage statement if you own property. Also fill in the lender's own Net Worth Worksheet.
  • [ ] Photo ID: required for Futurpreneur's MyFP portal and other online application portals.
  • [ ] Articles of Incorporation: once the company exists.
  • [ ] Land lease: a conditional or signed land lease with the landlord. Or a strong letter of intent, if the lease is not final yet.
  • [ ] Builder quotes: written quotes from your builder RFQ, Part 4 above. The lender treats these as your equipment purchase order.
  • [ ] Insurance: proof of insurance, or a quote for it. A CSBFP loan requires insurance on the financed equipment.
  • [ ] Alterna extra: 6 months of your own bank statements, plus a short interview.

Detail: lenders judge a loan partly by DSCR, the debt service coverage ratio. Most want 1.20 to 1.25 or higher. A CSBFP loan can also require a personal guarantee, which can be set as high as the full loan amount.

RULE: most lenders count your own cash, not borrowed money, as equity. Start with money already in a Canadian bank account with a clear history. It is the cleanest, cheapest and fastest cash a lender will accept.

1.5 Richmond Hill, the critical path, detail

Section 7.6.7 reads, quoted directly from the April 2026 consolidation: "A Ground Sign may be used for Off-Premises Advertising, provided such Sign: is only erected on lands Zoned for Commercial or Industrial use; is not located within 100 metres of any lands Zoned for Residential use unless functioning as an Electric Vehicle Charging Station Sign; is not located within 300 metres of another Sign containing Off-Premises Advertising unless functioning as an Electric Vehicle Charging Station Sign; and is not located within 400 metres of any Provincial Highway." The critical open question. The Sign By-law never defines "Commercial Zone," "Industrial Zone" or "Residential Zone." Richmond Hill's new comprehensive zoning by-law, CZBL 93-25, passed September 24 2025 with an Ontario Land Tribunal decision on July 2 2026, replaced the old zone names with mixed-use categories: RMU-COR1 and RMU-COR2, Regional Mixed Use Corridor, KDA-YB and KDA-YC, Yonge corridor Key Development Areas, and RHC, all of which permit both Commercial uses and Apartment Dwellings in the same zone. No City mapping, FAQ, staff report or memo answers whether a mixed-use zone counts as Zoned for Commercial use, which would allow a sign, or as containing land Zoned for Residential use, which could block every neighbouring lot within 100 m under the second test. A City staff memo dated September 22 2025 confirms Industrial lands remain governed by the older by-laws, since Employment Area lands were deliberately left out of CZBL 93-25. Judgment, not fact: our estimate is an 85 to 90 percent chance the City accepts the reading favourable to a sign. On the unfavourable reading, zero of the ordinary shortlisted lots pass. The only route to certainty is a written answer per parcel. Three free or low-cost channels exist: a Request for Zoning By-law Information, up to 5 business days, building@richmondhill.ca, 905-771-8810, a Permitted Use Letter through the permit portal, and a written sign by-law interpretation from signpermits@richmondhill.ca.

Face size. A true 10 ft by 20 ft LED face is 18.58 m2. Richmond Hill's 19.00 m2 cap on Commercial land counts the sign's frame, so a real 10x20 board with a normal bezel is over the limit. Two fixes exist: specify a bezel no wider than 22.8 mm, or build an 8 ft by 16 ft face instead, 11.9 m2, which also needs only about 40 m of frontage rather than 63 m and saves an estimated $44,000 on the screen. Decisions.md item 4.

No-digital zones. The Downtown Special Signage Area bars any Digital Display entirely, section 9.1.1(c). Within it, the Village Core, Yonge corridor from about Major Mackenzie Drive to Dunlop and Wright Streets, caps a ground sign at 2.40 m2, and the Uptown and Civic Centre districts cap it at 1.00 m2, both non-digital, per Table E and Table F. The Headford area and any designated Heritage Property also bar Digital Display. The precise Schedule B and D maps are not inside the 46-page consolidation, so a parcel-level check against the City's own map is required before relying on a lot being outside these areas.

Rule-change risk. Richmond Hill's own 2026 resident survey found 63 percent of 246 respondents concerned about more signs, alongside 41 percent support for commercial ground signs. A Phase 3 comprehensive rewrite of the whole Sign By-law is planned with no date set. A new Council is sworn in November 15 2026. Our estimate is a 30 to 45 percent chance of tightening rules by 2028, with a 5 to 15 percent chance of forced removal carrying an estimated $157,000 stranded cost. Mitigation: permit-loss exit clause in every site lease (section 5).

1.6 East Gwillimbury detail

By-law 2026-077, enacted July 23 2026, cut the spacing test from 600 m to 300 m, section 18.1(a)(iv) as amended, added a 100 m intersection setback to the Part 18.2 electronic list, and split "Billboard Sign" into a static definition, excluding electronic, and a new "Electronic Billboard Sign" definition. Electronic specifications, Part 19: minimum 10 second message duration, maximum 1 second transition, 5,000 nits by day and 300 nits by night, no more than 3 lux above ambient within 10 m, no more than a 25 percent brightness change at a transition, dark from 23:00 to 07:00 if visible from a habitable room of a legal dwelling, automatic shutoff on malfunction, and third-party certification from an electronic sign expert such as the manufacturer. Four Village Cores, Queensville, Sharon, Mount Albert and Holland Landing, carry a 250 m halo for static billboards and are closed entirely to electronic signs within a 100 m halo.

Two traps. First, the Town's own PDF is not a true consolidation: pages 1 to 39 still carry the pre-amendment October 2025 text, including the old 600 m figure, and the amending By-law 2026-077 is stapled on as pages 44 to 47, last modified August 4 2026. Permit renewal is not a right. 1.7 Bradford West Gwillimbury and the Simcoe County layer

1.10 Known drafting traps in the primary sources

East Gwillimbury's public PDF is not a consolidated document and displays the pre-amendment 600 m figure on most of its pages, with the actual 300 m amendment stapled on at the end. East Gwillimbury's amendment also creates an ambiguity over whether the new 100 m intersection setback reaches a static Billboard Sign or only an Electronic Billboard Sign. Richmond Hill's Sign By-law never defines the zone names its own tests depend on, and the City's new comprehensive zoning by-law never mentions the Sign By-law at all. Newmarket's section 3.9 cross-references York Region's since-repealed By-law 2015-36, whose Schedule A survives only by an express saving clause in the replacement By-law 2025-62, and the list it points to is a list of where regional-road temporary signs may be placed, not a list of prohibited intersections, so Newmarket's own prohibition-style wording appears to invert the source list's meaning. Newmarket's by-law also contradicts itself on digital signs: section 25.1 permits Electronic Changeable Copy on the whole face of a billboard, but section 16.6 elsewhere says a billboard may be illuminated but not electrically animated and may not have automated message changes. Read together, section 25 likely governs a digital face in practice, and the Town's own staff report on the 749 Davis Drive application treated that sign as having a digital face, but the Town has not confirmed this reading in writing. Toronto's Chapter 694 text contains a literal drafting error stating an electronic ground sign's height limit in square metres rather than metres.

2. Provincial and Toronto layers

2.1 The permit stack, in order

A billboard needs approvals from up to eleven separate authorities depending on the parcel. In order: landowner authorization by option or lease. Written zoning confirmation, essential in Richmond Hill given the ambiguity above. The town's sign permit or licence. A building permit under the Ontario Building Code, since a freestanding sign of this size is a designated structure requiring engineer-stamped drawings and a post-erection engineer's report. Site plan control approval, where the property is subject to it. York Region Corridor Control, if the frontage is a Regional Road, Yonge Street is Regional Road 1, Davis Drive is Regional Road 31, Green Lane is Regional Road 19, Leslie Street is Regional Road 12, Woodbine Avenue is Regional Road 8. The relevant conservation authority, the Toronto and Region Conservation Authority for Richmond Hill, if the parcel sits in a regulated area. An MTO permit, if any part of the sign is within 400 m of a provincial highway. Simcoe County's own permit, if the Bradford parcel is within 400 m of a County Road. The electricity utility's offer to connect. An Electrical Safety Authority inspection and Certificate of Acceptance before energization. A municipal business licence is generally not required for a sign or advertising company in any of the towns checked.

2.2 MTO and the Public Transportation and Highway Improvement Act

The Highway Corridor Management Manual, April 2025 edition, remains current, confirmed against the Ministry's own library record updated August 31 2026. Section 5.7 states: "Billboard signs are not permitted within the controlled area adjacent to Class 1 and 2 highways." Sections 5.7.2 and 5.7.3 apply this to both rural and built-up or urban segments alike, so there is no urban exception. Class 1 and 2 highways cover the entire 400-series network. The statute, the Public Transportation and Highway Improvement Act, section 38(2)(e), requires a Ministry permit for any sign within 400 m of a controlled-access highway's limit, and section 38(11) lets the Minister cancel any such permit at any time at the Minister's discretion. A November 27 2025 amendment, now section 96, bars any claim for compensation arising from a highway designation, a section 34 or 38 prohibition or direction, or a permit refusal or cancellation. A billboard permit fee is $770, valid 5 years, with a 30 business day combined acknowledgment and decision window, applied for through the Highway Corridor Management System at hcms.mto.gov.on.ca. Contact, MTO Central Region, Downsview, 416-235-5385.

One frequently repeated sentence does not appear in the April 2025 manual and should not be quoted: that the 400 m band is measured from the projected mainline right-of-way at an interchange. No such sentence was found on a direct search of the current text.

2.3 Highway 407 Act

The controlling provision is section 35(3): "The Minister of Transportation shall control the use of the lands adjacent to the Highway 407 lands, and section 38 of the Public Transportation and Highway Improvement Act applies to the adjacent lands." Highway 407 crosses Yonge Street in the Vaughan and Markham area roughly 150 m south of the Yonge and Highway 7 intersection, so the excluded 400 m band on that stretch of Yonge runs from about Highway 7 to about 8790 Yonge Street, not roughly 3 km.

2.4 Freeway board tracing

Every freeway-adjacent billboard our research could trace to a public record sits on Metrolinx, ONroute or reserve land under an express Minister's Order, most visibly the June 15 2023 Order permitting new billboards at 23 ONroute locations on Highways 400 and 401. One board could not be traced: RCC Media announced a new digital billboard at Highway 400 and Steeles Avenue West on October 28 2025, but no address, side of the road, owner, size or permit record was found on either the Toronto or the Vaughan side of that intersection, and it does not appear on RCC's own locations page. Claim only that every freeway board we could trace sits on government or reserve land under a Minister's Order. The Highway 400 and Steeles board is unresolved as of September 21 2026.

2.5 Conservation authorities and Regional roads

The Toronto and Region Conservation Authority covers Richmond Hill's regulated areas. Its fee schedule, effective February 1 2026, lists two figures for an ancillary structure permit, $1,035 and $2,555 per component, but the schedule's own column layout makes it unclear which figure applies to a minor single structure outside all hazards and which applies to a fuller commercial structure. Ask TRCA directly, 416-661-6600 or info@trca.ca, before budgeting this line. The Lake Simcoe Region Conservation Authority governs East Gwillimbury and Bradford's regulated areas, with a Minor permit at $2,625, an Intermediate permit at $4,171 and a Major permit at $6,300, effective April 1 2026. York Region's Signs on Regional Roads By-law 2025-62, in force January 1 2026, governs only structures placed in, on or over a Regional road allowance, so a sign kept entirely on private land does not need Regional approval under this by-law, though any work inside the road allowance, such as a new driveway or utility connection, needs a separate Regional encroachment permit with an estimated 8-week lead time.

2.6 City of Toronto, Chapter 694

Chapter 694, section 694-24A, bans a new third-party sign within 400 m of Highways 400, 401, 404, 409, and 427 for the segments listed, the Don Valley Parkway, the Gardiner Expressway and the Queen Elizabeth Way, plus a 450 m band either side of Yonge Street from Highway 401 north to Drewry Avenue on the west side and Cummer Avenue on the east side. Outside those ban zones, a third-party sign is permitted only in Commercial districts, wall signs only, Commercial Residential districts, a small wall sign only, Employment districts, ground and electronic ground, and Utility districts, ground, wall, electronic ground and electronic wall, capped at 20.0 m2 per face.

Toronto's Third Party Sign Tax adds a second, independent cost on top of any permit. An electronic ground sign with an aggregate face area over 5 m2 and up to 45 m2, which covers a standard 10x20 board, falls in Class IV at $15,084.23 a year, confirmed against the City's own 2026 rate page. Rates increase annually, invoices are assessed by March 31 and payment is due July 1.

The Sign Variance Committee's full record from February 2024 through September 18 2026 shows 28 decisions on third-party electronic sign applications, 10 granted and 18 refused, a 64 percent refusal rate. Every successful applicant brought a staff-supported, near-compliant proposal in an Employment or Commercial district and paid the $2,039.44 variance fee. JOLT Charge, an EV-charging and digital advertising company backed by a $194 million Canada Infrastructure Bank partnership, won 5 of its 13 Toronto applications, including two inside a highway ban zone. Pattison won 1 of its 5 attempts, at 4711 Steeles Avenue East, and was refused the other 4, most recently on September 18 2026 for a proposed sign at 3474 Kingston Road. Toronto is closed by its by-law geometry and its sign tax, not by a Pattison monopoly. A variance is the only route, and it is costly and uncertain, open only to a well-funded, patient applicant.

5. Company, tax, immigration and contract layer

Corporate structure. An Ontario corporation under the Business Corporations Act is the recommended vehicle for a two-owner business, at $300 to incorporate online, with a written unanimous shareholder agreement. This avoids the federal Canada Business Corporations Act's rule that at least 25 percent of directors, or at least one director if there are fewer than four, must be a resident Canadian, a rule that can catch a permanent resident once more than one year has passed since that person became eligible to apply for citizenship. Ontario's own director-residency requirement was repealed in 2021 and does not apply. Directors remain personally liable for up to six months of unpaid wages and up to twelve months of unpaid vacation pay under the Business Corporations Act, and can be held liable for unremitted HST or payroll source deductions, so a shareholder agreement should not be treated as full personal-liability protection.

Your employment. The Employment Standards Act, section 67.2, voids a non-compete agreement for any employee who is not an executive as the Act narrowly defines that term. Check whether your own job title meets that narrow definition. If it does not, no statutory non-compete bars a side business here. The common-law duty of loyalty and good-faith dealing during employment still applies with no written agreement in place. The practical steps are to do all billboard work on personal time with personal equipment, never use your employer's customer data, disclose the side business to your manager in writing and obtain written acknowledgment, and avoid selling advertising space to your employer's direct competitors without separate disclosure and consent.

Lease and contract essentials. A lease over three years should have a notice of lease registered on title under the Land Titles Act, section 111, to bind a future buyer or mortgagee of the land. Land transfer tax on a lease is avoided only where the unexpired term, counting every renewal and option, cannot exceed 50 years in total. Every advertiser insertion order should include a right to reject or remove non-compliant content, a publisher-not-liable clause, an indemnity running from the advertiser, and payment terms tied to posting. Competition Act section 52 reaches a publisher who knowingly runs a misleading ad (see Other publisher duties). Every site lease should include a permit-loss exit clause given the rule-change risk described above.

HST and tax. Register for HST voluntarily on day one, since the roughly 13 percent paid on the structure, screen, installation and legal fees becomes a recoverable input tax credit once registered, and elect monthly filing at registration. A refund is normally issued within about 4 weeks, though a large first refund can trigger a review adding an estimated 8 to 10 weeks. Combined federal and Ontario small business corporate tax on the first $500,000 of active income is 11.2 percent from July 1 2026 onward, blending to about 11.7 percent for a full 2026 calendar year corporation. The structure and screen fall in Capital Cost Allowance Class 8, a 20 percent declining balance, with an enhanced first-year deduction available for assets acquired before 2030.

Owner pay and family tax rules. Plan to pay nobody in year one except the documented repayment of money the owners themselves lent to the company. The Tax on Split Income rules apply to both owners whenever co-founders are related by marriage or blood, and a family member can only qualify for the excluded-business exception by working an average of 20 hours a week with contemporaneous timesheets. Take no dividends until an accountant has reviewed the structure.

Immigration status. If your co-founder is not a Canadian citizen or permanent resident, get their immigration status confirmed in writing, by a licensed immigration consultant (RCIC) or lawyer, before they do any paid or unpaid work in the company. Under the federal Immigration and Refugee Protection Regulations, section 2, "work" includes any paid activity and can also include unpaid activity that directly competes with citizens or permanent residents in the labour market, so unpaid day-to-day operating work can still count as work requiring status that permits it. A Canadian citizen or a permanent resident who maintains the 730-day residency obligation may own shares, direct, work in and be paid by the company without restriction. An open work permit holder may work for any employer, but Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada's own public pages do not say whether that extends to being self-employed, and a written opinion from an RCIC or immigration lawyer is needed before relying on it. An employer-specific work permit holder may only work for the named employer, and a study permit holder is capped at 24 hours a week during term time, with self-employment income counting toward that cap. A visitor, including a business visitor, may not work in the Canadian labour market at all. Confirm which document they hold, a permanent resident card, a work permit with its stated conditions, or a study permit. The Start-up Visa program and the federal Self-Employed Persons Program are both currently paused and are not a live option.

FINTRAC and money services businesses. Any business that advertises foreign exchange or money transfer services, including by an interior or exterior sign, is itself treated as a money services business and must be registered with FINTRAC, whether or not it conducts transactions. RULE: verify every currency-exchange advertiser's FINTRAC registration number against the public MSB registry before signing a contract and again before each renewal, since a spot check during this research found one previously assumed advertiser's public registry record showing an expired registration, and at least 18 MSB registrations on Yonge Street were revoked at some point during 2026. Cap currency exchanges at 2 of the 6 slots on any one board regardless of registration status, to avoid the loop reading as a remittance network.

Other publisher duties. The Municipal Elections Act and the Election Finances Act both impose a written-information duty on a billboard company running a third-party political advertisement, plus a 4-year record-keeping and public-inspection duty at the municipal level and a 2-year duty at the provincial level. The Competition Act's misleading-advertising provisions, section 52, expressly reach a publisher who knowingly permits a misleading representation to run, not only the advertiser who created it, and carry penalties up to $10 million or three times the benefit obtained, whichever is greater, for a corporation. Cannabis and tobacco or vaping advertising is barred outright under their respective federal Acts. Liquor advertising should stay at least 200 m from a school under an AGCO guideline, which uses "should not" wording rather than a hard statutory ban.
